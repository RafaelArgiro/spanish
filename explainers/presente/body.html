0What the Present Tense Is Used ForEl presente — usos

The Spanish present covers more ground than the English simple present. One Spanish form does the work of several English ones, which is why a literal translation often sounds wrong in one direction or the other.

The five main uses

UseExampleEnglish
Habitual actionsTrabajo desde casa.I work from home.
Happening right now
no progressive needed		¿Qué haces? — Leo.What are you doing? — I'm reading.
The near future
with a time expression		Mañana salgo a las ocho.Tomorrow I leave at eight.
Ongoing situations
English uses the perfect		Hace tres años que vivo aquí.I have lived here for three years.
Narrating the past vividly
historical present		En 1492 Colón llega a América.In 1492 Columbus arrives in America.

Subject pronouns are usually dropped

The ending already tells you who the subject is. Hablo can only mean “I speak”, so yo is left out unless you are contrasting or emphasising.

1Regular VerbsVerbos regulares

Every Spanish infinitive ends in -ar, -er, or -ir. To conjugate a regular verb you remove those last two letters to get the stem — hablar → habl-, comer → com-, abrir → abr- — then add the ending for the person you want.

The three sets of endings

Person -ARhablar · to speak -ERcomer · to eat -IRabrir · to open

ellos / ellas / ustedeshablancomenabren
The single most useful observation

-ER and -IR verbs are identical except in the nosotros and vosotros forms, marked in gold above. Those two forms are the only place in the whole present tense where the -er/-ir distinction shows up at all.

Hablo español con mi familia.I speak Spanish with my family. Comemos a la una.We eat at one. Abren la tienda a las nueve.They open the shop at nine.

Points worth fixing in memory

  • The yo form always ends in -o for regular verbs, no matter the group.
  • The vosotros form always carries a written accent: habláis, coméis, abrís.
  • The nosotros form of -ar and -ir verbs (hablamos, abrimos) is identical to the preterite form. Context tells you which one is meant.

2Stem-Changing VerbsVerbos con cambio en la raíz

In these verbs the endings stay completely regular. What changes is the last vowel of the stem, and only when that vowel is stressed.

The stress falls on the stem in four of the six forms: yo, tú, él, ellos. It falls on the ending in nosotros and vosotros. So the change happens in four forms and not in the other two.

yochanged
túchanged
él / ella / ustedchanged
nosotrosunchanged
vosotrosunchanged
ellos / ellas / ustedeschanged
Laid out in a column, the changed forms wrap around the unchanged middle in an L-shape. This is why they are commonly called boot verbs or shoe verbs. The same shape governs every pattern in this section.
A stem change cannot be predicted

Nothing in the infinitive tells you whether a verb changes its stem. Dictionaries mark it explicitly, for example pensar (ie) or dormir (ue). Learn the marking together with the verb.

Most -iar verbs take no accent at all

The majority of -iar verbs are simply regular. Compare:

  • estudiar → estudio, estudias, estudia  —  no accent
  • cambiar → cambio, cambias, cambia  —  no accent
  • enviar → envío, envías, envía  —  accent

There is no rule to tell these apart. They have to be learned individually.

3Irregular YO FormsPrimera persona irregular

These verbs are regular in five of the six forms. Only the yo form is irregular, and it is irregular for a reason: either the spelling has to change to keep the pronunciation consistent before the -o ending, or the verb simply takes an extra letter.

Changes stack

If a verb also has a stem change from section 2, that stem change still applies on top of the irregular yo form. Seguir is both e → i and a -guir verb; elegir is both e → i and g → j.

4Fully Irregular VerbsVerbos irregulares

These twelve change in more than just the yo form. They are also the most frequently used verbs in the language, so the effort of memorising them pays off immediately.

Ser, estar and ir

The three whose present forms barely resemble their infinitive. Ir in particular comes from a different Latin verb, which is why it shares no letters with its own infinitive.

Estar carries a written accent on every form except yo and nosotros.

Soy de Bélgica. Ella es ingeniera.I am from Belgium. She is an engineer. Estoy cansado. El libro está en la mesa.I am tired. The book is on the table.

ir + a + infinitive

This is the standard way to express the near future.

Voy a estudiar esta noche.I am going to study tonight.

Tener, querer and decir

All three are stem-changing. Tener and decir add the irregular -go yo form on top; querer is a plain boot verb in the present and only turns irregular in other tenses (quise, querré).

Tener where English uses “to be”

Spanish uses tener for age, hunger, thirst, cold, fear and sleepiness: Tengo treinta años (I am thirty), tengo hambre (I am hungry), tengo frío (I am cold).

Haber — to have (auxiliary) · there is, there are

Haber is almost never used on its own. It has two jobs:

  1. Auxiliary for the perfect tense, always followed by a past participle.
  2. Impersonal “there is / there are”, in the special form hay, which never changes for number.

He comido. ¿Has visto la película?I have eaten. Have you seen the film?Hay un problema. Hay tres sillas.There is a problem. There are three chairs.

5Reflexive VerbsVerbos reflexivos

A reflexive verb is one where the subject and the object are the same person. The infinitive is marked with -se on the end: lavarse, levantarse, llamarse.

A collision worth knowing

Two of these produce the same yo form and have to be told apart by context:

  • me siento from sentarse — I sit down
  • me siento from sentirse — I feel

Me siento en la silla porque no me siento bien.I sit down on the chair because I do not feel well.

Why the accents appear

When a pronoun is attached to a gerund or a command, the stress of the original word does not move. A written accent is added to preserve it: duchándome, dúchate, levántate, siéntese.

Body parts and clothing take the definite article

Where English uses a possessive, Spanish uses el / la / los / las. The reflexive pronoun already tells you whose it is.

Me lavo las manos.I wash my hands. Se quita el abrigo.He takes off his coat. Nos cepillamos los dientes.We brush our teeth.

The pronoun can also be reciprocal

In the plural, nos and se can mean “each other” instead of “oneself”.

Nos vemos mañana.We'll see each other tomorrow. Se escriben todas las semanas.They write to each other every week.

The reflexive form can change the meaning

For many verbs, adding the pronoun is not just a grammatical detail; it produces a different verb.

Some verbs exist in no other form: arrepentirse (to regret), atreverse (to dare), quejarse (to complain), suicidarse.
